# Probe Intended Decisions

## Why this skill exists

Clutterbuck (2004) described the sounding board role as a distinctive mentor
function: the mentor helps the mentee analyse their own decisions and choices
by reflecting them back with probing questions, rather than offering advice or
opinions. This is different from the critical-friend role (which challenges
specific assumptions) and from coaching (which builds toward a goal). The
sounding board is used when the learner has already formed an intention and
needs to think it through rigorously before acting — not to be told whether
it is right, but to be helped to examine it.

For an educational agent, this skill is relevant in educator and mentor
contexts: a learner has made a pedagogical decision (e.g. how to structure a
lesson, which method to use, how to handle a difficult student situation) and
wants to stress-test it through dialogue.

**Research grounding:**
Clutterbuck, D. (2004). *Everyone Needs a Mentor*. CIPD.
Clutterbuck, D. (2015). In *Coaching and Mentoring*. Routledge.

---

## When to use this skill

- The learner has a plan or decision and says so explicitly.
- The learner asks "What do you think of my plan?" — but benefits more
  from examining it themselves than from being told.
- The learner is about to execute a plan in a domain where the agent has
  limited ability to judge correctness (context, values, and priorities
  belong to the learner).
- The learner's decision involves trade-offs or priorities — not a single
  right answer.

## When NOT to use this skill

- The decision is factually wrong and correction is more efficient than
  reflection. Use identify-misconceptions or provide-formative-feedback instead.
- The learner needs information they do not have — give the information first,
  then probe.
- The learner has explicitly asked for advice or a recommendation, not a
  sounding board. Honour the request; do not substitute sounding-board
  behaviour when advice was asked for.

---

## Sounding board approach

The sounding board does not:
- Give its own opinion on the plan.
- Recommend a different course of action.
- Approve or validate the plan uncritically.
- Challenge the plan adversarially.

The sounding board does:
- Reflect the plan back in the learner's own terms.
- Ask questions that open up dimensions of the decision the learner may not
  have examined.
- Help the learner surface trade-offs, consequences, and alternatives.
- Return the evaluation to the learner.

---

## Procedure

### Step 1 — Reflect the decision back
Paraphrase the learner's plan in their terms, not yours. This confirms you
have understood it and signals that you are working with their framing.
"So your plan is: [paraphrase]. Is that right?"

### Step 2 — Open a dimension
Identify one dimension of the decision the learner has not addressed. Choose
from:

| Dimension | Probing question |
|-----------|-----------------|
| **Consequences** | "What happens if this goes as planned — and what happens if it doesn't?" |
| **Stakeholders** | "Who else is affected by this decision, and how might they experience it?" |
| **Trade-offs** | "What are you giving up by choosing this approach over another?" |
| **Assumptions** | "What would need to be true for this plan to work?" |
| **Timing** | "Is now the right time for this — what would change if you waited?" |
| **Alternatives** | "What other options did you consider, and what made you rule them out?" |
| **Purpose** | "What does success look like to you here — how will you know it worked?" |

Choose ONE dimension. Ask the question. Wait.

### Step 3 — Receive and reflect
After the learner responds, reflect their answer back:
"So you're saying that [summary]. Does that feel right when you hear it
back?"

Do not yet evaluate. The reflection step gives the learner the experience
of hearing their own reasoning at a slight remove — this often surfaces
things the learner had not noticed.

### Step 4 — Open a second dimension (if needed)
If the learner's examination of the first dimension has resolved their
thinking, move to a second unexamined dimension. Use the same approach.
Do not exceed two or three dimensions in one session — the goal is depth,
not coverage.

### Step 5 — Return evaluation to the learner
At the end, do not conclude with your assessment. Return it:
"Having gone through this — how does the plan look to you now? Would you
change anything?"

---

## Output format

**Reflection:**
```
[Paraphrase of the plan in the learner's terms]
[Confirmation question: "Is that right?"]
```

**First probe:**
```
[One question on one unexamined dimension]
```

**After learner responds:**
```
[Reflection of the learner's answer]
["Does that feel right when you hear it back?"]
```

**Final return:**
```
["Having thought through this — how does the plan look to you now?"]
```

---

## Success criteria

- The agent did not offer its own opinion or recommendation.
- The learner did the evaluative work — the agent provided the structure.
- The learner's understanding of their own plan deepened through the
  conversation.
- The evaluation was explicitly returned to the learner at the end.

## Failure modes to avoid

- **Advice-giving disguised as questions:** "Have you considered doing X
  instead?" — this is a recommendation in question form.
- **Approving the plan without examination:** "Sounds good to me!" — no
  sounding-board value.
- **Challenging the plan adversarially:** the sounding board is not a devil's
  advocate. It is genuinely trying to help the learner examine their own
  decision — not to argue them out of it.
- **Too many dimensions:** asking five probing questions in a row overwhelms
  the learner. One or two, with depth.

---

## Example

**Scenario:** An educator-role learner says: "I've decided to use a
problem-based learning activity for my class next week. The students will work
in groups on a real-world problem. I think this will work well."

**Step 1 — Reflect:**
> "So your plan is to run a group problem-based learning activity next week,
> using a real-world problem. And your instinct is that this will work well
> for this group — is that a fair summary?"

**Step 2 — Probe (Assumptions dimension):**
> "What would need to be true about the students for this to go well?
> What are you assuming they can do independently in a group without your
> direction?"

**Step 3 — Reflect:**
*Learner says: "I'm assuming they can manage the group process and stay
on task."*
> "So the plan depends on the students being able to self-manage in groups.
> Does that feel like a safe assumption when you hear it out loud — or is
> it something you might need to plan for?"

**Step 5 — Return:**
> "Having thought about this — does the plan still feel right, or is there
> anything you'd build in differently?"
