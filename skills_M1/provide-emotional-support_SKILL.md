# Provide Emotional Support

## Why this skill exists

Kram (1983) identified two categories of mentor function. Career functions
(coaching, sponsorship, challenge) develop competence. Psychosocial functions
develop the learner's sense of identity and confidence. The counselling
function — providing a safe space for anxieties and fears — is one of the
most important psychosocial functions because without it, learners disengage
before competence can develop. Hattie (2009) also found teacher-student
relationships to have a significant effect on learning (d=0.72). An agent that
ignores or dismisses emotional signals from learners undermines the relational
foundation that makes instruction effective.

This skill does not replace professional mental health support. It addresses
normal learning-related anxiety, frustration, and self-doubt — the emotional
experience that is inherent to learning hard things.

**Research grounding:**
Kram, K. E. (1983). Phases of the mentor relationship.
*Academy of Management Journal, 26*(4), 608–625.
Kram, K. E. (1985). *Mentoring at Work*. Scott Foresman.
Hattie, J. (2009). *Visible Learning*. Routledge.

---

## When to use this skill

- Learner expresses negative self-evaluation ("I'm terrible at this",
  "I'll never get it").
- Learner expresses anxiety before assessment or a difficult task.
- Learner has made an error and appears discouraged rather than curious.
- Learner talks about giving up.
- Learner is visibly comparing themselves unfavourably to others.

## When NOT to use this skill

- The learner's emotional state is not visible in the interaction — do not
  project emotions that have not been expressed.
- The learner has expressed distress that suggests something beyond normal
  learning anxiety (crisis, clinical concern) — do not continue as if
  this skill is sufficient. Acknowledge and point toward appropriate support.
- The emotional expression is brief and the learner clearly wants to continue
  — a brief acknowledgement is enough; do not over-focus on the emotion.

---

## Procedure

### Step 1 — Acknowledge before anything else
Do not immediately move to reassurance, correction, or instruction. Name what
you observed: "It sounds like this is feeling really frustrating right now."
This is not agreement with the learner's self-assessment — it is acknowledgement
that the feeling is real.

**What to avoid:**
- "Don't worry, you'll be fine!" — dismisses the feeling.
- "Actually you're doing well" — sounds like contradiction before acknowledgement.
- Moving straight to "Let's try again" — ignores the emotional state entirely.

### Step 2 — Normalise the struggle
Make clear that the difficulty the learner is experiencing is a normal and
expected part of learning, not evidence that they cannot learn. Use a truthful,
specific normalisation — not a platitude.

**Effective normalisation:**
- "Most people find this specific step hard the first time — it goes against
  intuition."
- "The fact that it's frustrating is actually a signal that you're working at
  the right level. If it felt easy, we'd be wasting your time."
- Avoid: "Everyone feels this way sometimes" — too vague to be comforting.

### Step 3 — Reframe the error or difficulty
Where appropriate, reframe the learner's error or struggle as information
rather than failure. "This is useful data — you now know exactly where the
gap is, which is much better than not knowing."

### Step 4 — Ask what the learner needs
Do not assume what support is needed. Ask:
"Would it help to slow down and go back a step? Or would you rather try
again and see what happens?"
This returns agency to the learner.

### Step 5 — Reconnect to capability
Before returning to instruction, briefly reconnect to what the learner has
already achieved. This is not flattery — it is a factual reminder of prior
success that counterbalances the current discouragement.

### Step 6 — Offer to continue at the learner's pace
Do not push forward until the learner indicates they are ready.

---

## Output format

```
[Acknowledgement — name the feeling without dismissing it]

[Normalisation — specific, truthful, not a platitude]

[Reframe — error or difficulty as useful information]

[Question about what the learner needs]

[One reminder of prior capability — specific, not generic]

[Offer to continue when ready]
```

---

## Success criteria

- The learner's emotional state was acknowledged before any instruction
  resumed.
- The normalisation was specific to the difficulty, not a generic platitude.
- The learner was given a choice about how to proceed.
- Instruction did not resume until the learner indicated readiness.

## Failure modes to avoid

- **Premature reassurance:** "You'll be fine!" before the learner feels heard.
- **Contradiction:** "Actually, you're doing well" before acknowledgement —
  sounds like dismissal.
- **Skipping emotional acknowledgement to get back to content:** this
  communicates that feelings are irrelevant, which damages the learning
  relationship.
- **Over-dwelling:** spending so long on emotional support that the session
  never returns to learning. The goal is to stabilise and continue, not to
  provide extended counselling.
- **Empty praise:** "You're so smart" — not specific, not evidence-based,
  and can backfire if the learner does not believe it.

## Example

**Scenario:** After a second failed attempt at a programming task, learner
writes: "I don't know why I'm even trying. I'm just not a coder."

**Step 1 — Acknowledge:**
> "That's a really deflating feeling — putting in the effort twice and still
> not getting there. That makes sense."

**Step 2 — Normalise:**
> "For the record: this specific error — the one you keep hitting — is one
> that trips up people who've been coding for years. It's not a beginner error
> in the way it might feel."

**Step 3 — Reframe:**
> "Two attempts also means you now know what doesn't work. That's not nothing —
> you've narrowed down where the problem is, even if it doesn't feel that way."

**Step 4 — Ask:**
> "Would it help to step back and look at a simpler version of this problem
> first, or would you rather I walk through what happened in your last attempt
> with you?"

**Step 5 — Prior capability:**
> "You got the data structure right in the last task — that part you understood.
> This is the next layer on top of that."

**Step 6 — Offer:**
> "Let's take it at whatever pace you need."
