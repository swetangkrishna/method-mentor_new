# Provide Formative Feedback

## Why this skill exists

Hattie's (2009) meta-analysis of over 800 studies found feedback to be one of
the highest-impact influences on learning (d=0.73). But Hattie & Timperley
(2007) showed that not all feedback is equally effective. Simple "right/wrong"
or praise-only feedback has near-zero effect. Feedback is powerful only when it
is information-rich, targeted at the right level, and tells the learner
specifically what to do next. This skill applies their three-level feedback
model: task level, process level, and self-regulation level.

**Research grounding:**
Hattie, J. (2009). *Visible Learning*. Routledge.
Hattie, J., & Timperley, H. (2007). The power of feedback.
*Review of Educational Research, 77*(1), 81–112.

---

## When to use this skill

- The learner has produced an attempt — correct, incorrect, or partial.
- The goal is to improve performance, not to grade it.
- There is still an opportunity for the learner to use the feedback (i.e.,
  the task is not over).

## When NOT to use this skill

- The interaction is summative — the learner wants a final assessment, not
  improvement feedback.
- The learner's attempt is so incomplete that feedback is premature — ask for
  more work first.

---

## Feedback level selection

Choose the level based on where the learner currently is:

| Level | When to use | What it addresses |
|-------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Task level** | Learner has a factual error or misunderstood the task requirements | Whether the specific answer or product is correct |
| **Process level** | Learner has the right idea but used the wrong strategy or approach | The process or strategy used to complete the task |
| **Self-regulation level** | Learner is stuck on how to monitor, evaluate, or improve their own work | Metacognitive control — how the learner manages their own learning |

**Important:** Do not mix levels in one feedback response. Choose the level
where the learner needs help right now. Task-level feedback is appropriate for
early errors. Process-level feedback is appropriate once the task is understood.
Self-regulation feedback is appropriate when the learner needs to become more
independent.

---

## Feedback construction rules (all levels)

From Hattie & Timperley (2007), effective feedback answers three questions:

1. **Where am I going?** (Feed up) — What is the goal?
2. **How am I going?** (Feedback) — What is my current performance relative
   to the goal?
3. **Where to next?** (Feed forward) — What action should I take?

Every feedback response must answer question 3. Feedback that only answers
questions 1 and 2 is incomplete — it tells the learner where they are but not
how to move.

---

## Procedure

### Step 1 — Read the learner's attempt
Identify: what did the learner get right? What is the gap between their
current output and the success criteria?

### Step 2 — Select feedback level
Use the table above. Default to task level for first attempts; move to process
or self-regulation as the learner progresses.

### Step 3 — Acknowledge what is correct
Before addressing the gap, name what the learner did correctly. This is not
empty praise — it is information that tells the learner which parts of their
strategy to keep. Be specific: "Your opening paragraph clearly states the
claim" is useful; "Good work!" is not.

### Step 4 — Name the gap precisely
State specifically what is missing or wrong. Do not hedge with "maybe" or
"you might want to". Be direct.

### Step 5 — Give the feed-forward action
Tell the learner specifically what to do next. The action must be concrete
enough that the learner can act on it immediately without further guidance.

### Step 6 — Do not give the answer
Feed-forward points toward the action, not the solution. "Reconsider what
happens when n=0" is feed-forward. "The answer is 1 when n=0" is answer-giving,
which short-circuits learning.

---

## Output format

```
[What is correct — specific, not generic]

[What the gap is — precise, not hedged]

[Feed-forward: one concrete action the learner should take next]
```

---

## Success criteria

- The feedback names something specific the learner did correctly.
- The feedback names the gap precisely — not "this could be improved" but
  "the loop condition is wrong because...".
- The feed-forward action is concrete enough to act on without further help.
- The learner is not given the answer — they are pointed toward finding it.

## Failure modes to avoid

- **Generic praise only:** "Great effort!" — tells the learner nothing.
- **Feedback without feed-forward:** "This is wrong" — provides no path.
- **Giving the answer as feedback:** "The correct answer is X" — removes
  the learning opportunity.
- **Over-long feedback:** pointing out five things at once. Choose the most
  important gap and address that one.
- **Feedback on the wrong level:** giving task-level corrections when the
  learner's problem is a process or strategy issue.

---

## Example

**Scenario:** Learner is writing a paragraph argument for an essay. They wrote:

> "Social media is bad. It makes people sad and they spend too much time on it.
> This is why it should be regulated."

**Step 1 — Read attempt:**
Correct: has a claim (regulation) and some evidence (sadness, time). Gap:
evidence is asserted, not supported; argument jumps from evidence to conclusion
without reasoning.

**Step 2 — Level:** Process level. The task is understood; the strategy
(how to build an argument) is the problem.

**Step 3 — Acknowledge:**
> "You've got the three-part structure right — claim, evidence, conclusion.
> That's the right skeleton."

**Step 4 — Name the gap:**
> "The gap is that your evidence is asserted rather than supported. You say
> social media 'makes people sad' but a reader who disagrees will ask: how do
> you know? What's your source? Without that, the argument doesn't hold."

**Step 5 — Feed-forward:**
> "Pick one piece of your evidence and add: what would a researcher or study
> need to show for that claim to be true? Then see if you can reference anything
> you've encountered that shows it. Just one sentence of support for one claim."
