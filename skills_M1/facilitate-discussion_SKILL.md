# Facilitate Discussion

## Why this skill exists

Kolb et al. (2014) identified the facilitator role as one of four core educator
roles in their Educator Role Profile. In the experiential learning cycle, the
facilitator's job is to create the space for concrete experience and reflective
observation — the phases where the learner processes what they have encountered
before constructing abstract understanding. Hattie (2009) found classroom
discussion to have an effect size of d=0.82 — significantly higher than most
forms of direct instruction alone. Discussion works because articulating an
idea forces the learner to organise it; encountering another perspective forces
revision; defending a position forces examination of evidence.

An agent that facilitates rather than lectures makes the learner do this work.

**Research grounding:**
Kolb, A. Y., Kolb, D. A., Passarelli, A., & Sharma, G. (2014). On becoming
an experiential educator. *Simulation & Gaming, 45*(2), 204–234.
Hattie, J. (2009). *Visible Learning*. Routledge.

---

## When to use this skill

- The topic has more than one legitimate perspective or interpretation.
- The learner would benefit from articulating their current understanding
  (not just receiving new information).
- The learner has been passive — a discussion prompt will draw them in.
- After direct instruction — discussion consolidates what was taught.
- The learning objective includes "evaluate", "justify", "compare", or
  "reflect on".

## When NOT to use this skill

- The topic has a correct answer the learner needs before they can discuss
  it meaningfully (teach first, then discuss).
- The learner has no prior knowledge to draw on — discussion without any
  existing knowledge produces confusion, not learning.
- Time is very short and direct instruction is more efficient.

---

## Facilitator behaviours

These are actions the agent takes to enable discussion rather than replace it:

| Behaviour | What it looks like |
|-----------|-------------------|
| **Open questioning** | Ask questions with multiple valid answers: "What do you think is the most important factor here, and why?" |
| **Building** | Take the learner's contribution and extend it without correcting or redirecting: "You said X — what would happen if X were not the case?" |
| **Redirecting** | When the learner looks to the agent for the answer, redirect: "What do you think? What's your instinct?" |
| **Summarising** | Reflect the learner's position back to them: "So your view is that... — is that right?" |
| **Probing** | Push for depth: "What's your evidence for that?" / "Have you considered the case where...?" |
| **Withholding** | The most important behaviour: the agent does NOT give its own opinion or the correct answer while discussion is active. |

## Procedure

### Step 1 — Set the discussion question
Frame a question that is open, consequential, and anchored in the content.
The question must be one the learner can engage with using what they currently
know — it should not require knowledge they do not have.

**Good discussion question:** "We've looked at two explanation strategies.
Which one do you think would work better for a learner who keeps making the
same error, and why?"

**Poor discussion question:** "Do you understand the two strategies?"
(Closed. Yes/no. Produces no discussion.)

### Step 2 — Wait
After asking the question, wait. Do not rephrase or prompt until the learner
has had time to respond. Silence is productive — do not fill it.

### Step 3 — Build on the learner's response
Do not evaluate the response as right or wrong. Take it seriously and build:
- If the response is partially developed: "Say more about that — what makes
  you think X?"
- If the response is off-topic: gently redirect: "Interesting — how does that
  connect to [the question]?"
- If the response is strong: probe it: "What would someone who disagreed with
  you say?"

### Step 4 — Introduce a challenge or counter-perspective
Once the learner has expressed a position, introduce a counter-case. Do not
assert the counter as correct — present it as a perspective to consider:
"What about the case where [Y]? Does your answer still hold?"

### Step 5 — Summarise and synthesise
At the end of the discussion, summarise what the learner has concluded in
their own terms. Ask them to do this if possible:
"So, having thought through this — what's your current view?"

### Step 6 — Connect back to learning objective
Return to the learning objective and note how the discussion addressed it.
This closes the loop and makes the learning visible.

## Output format

**Opening question:**
```
[One open, consequential question anchored in the content]
```

**Building response (after learner speaks):**
```
[Acknowledge the contribution without evaluating it]
[Building question or probing question]
```

**Synthesis (end of discussion):**
```
[Summary of learner's developed position in their terms]
[Connection back to the learning objective]
```

---

## Success criteria

- The learner spoke more than the agent during the discussion.
- The agent did not give its own opinion or the correct answer during the
  discussion phase.
- The learner's position developed or deepened between their first and last
  contribution.
- The discussion ended with a connection to the learning objective.

## Failure modes to avoid

- **Asking a closed question and calling it discussion:** "Do you agree with
  X?" is not a discussion prompt.
- **Answering the question yourself:** the agent gives its view, the learner
  agrees, discussion over. Nothing was learned.
- **Evaluating each learner contribution:** "Good point", "Not quite" — this
  turns discussion into Q&A with the agent holding the answer.
- **Filling silence:** if the learner takes time to think, let them. Do not
  rephrase the question or add hints before the learner has responded.

## Example

**Scenario:** Learner has just been taught two feedback strategies —
task-level feedback and process-level feedback — and the agent wants to
consolidate this through discussion.

**Step 1 — Opening question:**
> "You've seen both types. Imagine you're tutoring someone who keeps getting
> the same type of maths problem wrong. Which kind of feedback — task-level
> or process-level — would you give first, and what's your reasoning?"

**Step 3 — Build:**
*Learner says: "I'd give task-level first, to correct the wrong answer."*
> "Okay — so you'd start by telling them what the right answer is. What do
> you think happens when they encounter the same problem type next week —
> will they get it right then?"

**Step 4 — Challenge:**
> "What if the learner doesn't actually care about the answer to this
> specific problem — they just want to stop making this type of mistake?
> Does task-level feedback still come first?"

**Step 5 — Synthesis:**
> "So what's your position now — having thought through both cases?"
