# Set Learning Objectives

## Why this skill exists

Hattie (2009) identified teacher clarity — including the clear communication
of learning intentions and success criteria — as one of the highest-impact
instructional practices (d=0.75). When learners know what they are supposed to
learn and what success looks like, they can direct their own attention, monitor
their own progress, and engage more productively with feedback. Vague or absent
objectives produce unfocused effort. This skill operationalises teacher clarity
as a specific agent behaviour at the start of instruction.

**Research grounding:**
Hattie, J. (2009). *Visible Learning*. Routledge.
Hattie, J. (2012). *Visible Learning for Teachers*. Routledge.

---

## When to use this skill

- Beginning of a lesson, tutoring session, or structured explanation.
- When a learner asks "what are we doing today?" or equivalent.
- When prior interactions have been unfocused or the learner seems unsure
  what they are working toward.
- When designing a task — the objective must be set before the task is given.

## When NOT to use this skill

- Mid-session, unless the session has gone off-track and needs reorientation.
- When the learner has already stated a clear, specific goal themselves —
  adopt their framing rather than imposing a new one.

---

## Procedure

### Step 1 — State the learning intention
Write a single sentence that completes: "By the end of this, you will be able
to [verb] [content]."

**Verb selection:** Use a verb that describes visible, testable performance.
Avoid "understand", "know", or "appreciate". Prefer:
- Tutor context: explain, solve, identify, apply, debug, compare, predict.
- Teacher context: design, construct, evaluate, select, justify, plan.
- Educator context: analyse, critique, synthesise, align, reflect on.

**Content specification:** Name the specific topic, not a general domain.
"Solve quadratic equations using factorisation" not "learn about algebra".

### Step 2 — State the success criteria
Write 2–3 specific, observable indicators that would tell both the learner
and the agent that the objective has been met. These are the standards the
learner's output will be held against.

**Success criteria format:**
> "You'll know you've got it when you can: [criterion 1], [criterion 2],
> [criterion 3]."

Each criterion should be binary — the learner either meets it or does not.

### Step 3 — Connect to prior knowledge
Briefly anchor the objective to something the learner already knows. One
sentence. This activates prior knowledge (Hattie: prior achievement d=0.67)
and reduces the learner's cognitive load when encountering new content.

### Step 4 — Check the objective is shared
Ask one question to confirm the learner has understood what they are working
toward. This is not a comprehension check on content — it is a check that
the goal is clear.

---

## Output format

```
Today's goal: [Learning intention — one sentence, verb + specific content]

You'll know you've got it when you can:
1. [Success criterion 1]
2. [Success criterion 2]
3. [Success criterion 3]

This builds on [one sentence connecting to prior knowledge].

Before we start — [one question confirming the goal is clear to the learner].
```

---

## Success criteria for this skill

- The learning intention uses a testable verb.
- The success criteria are observable — a third party could judge whether
  each one is met.
- The learner has confirmed they understand what they are working toward.
- The objective is specific enough that by the end of the session it is
  clear whether it was achieved.

## Failure modes to avoid

- **"Understand" objectives:** "You will understand how photosynthesis works."
  "Understand" is not observable. Replace with "explain", "diagram", "predict".
- **Over-broad objectives:** "Learn Python." Narrow to one skill.
- **Success criteria that are just restatements of the objective:**
  "You'll know you got it when you understand photosynthesis" — circular.
- **Skipping the check:** stating the objective but not confirming the learner
  has registered it.

## Example

**Scenario:** Learner wants help with "writing better essays".

**Step 1 — Learning intention:**
> "Today's goal: by the end of this session, you'll be able to write a
> paragraph that makes one claim, supports it with one piece of evidence, and
> explains why the evidence supports the claim."

**Step 2 — Success criteria:**
> "You'll know you've got it when you can:
> 1. Identify the claim, evidence, and reasoning in a paragraph you haven't
>    written yourself.
> 2. Write a paragraph that has all three parts.
> 3. Spot a paragraph that is missing one of the three parts and name which
>    one is missing."

**Step 3 — Prior knowledge connection:**
> "You already know how to form sentences — we're just adding structure to
> group them into an argument."

**Step 4 — Check:**
> "In your own words — what are the three things a strong paragraph needs
> to have, based on what I just said?"
