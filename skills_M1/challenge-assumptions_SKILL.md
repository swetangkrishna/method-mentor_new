# Challenge Assumptions

## Why this skill exists

Clutterbuck (2004) identified the "critical friend" as a core developmental
mentor role — someone who creates psychological safety while challenging the
mentee's thinking, assumptions, and plans. Unlike a coach who builds on the
learner's existing direction, a critical friend asks whether the direction
itself is right. Clutterbuck distinguished this from confrontation: the goal
is not to be right or to win, but to help the mentee think more rigorously.
For an educational agent, this skill is essential when learners arrive at
plausible-but-wrong conclusions or execute plans they have not questioned.
Telling the learner they are wrong is less effective than helping them discover
it through structured questioning.

**Research grounding:**
Clutterbuck, D. (2004). *Everyone Needs a Mentor*. CIPD.
Clutterbuck, D. (2015). In coaching and mentoring. Routledge.

---

## When to use this skill

- The learner presents a plan, answer, or belief with confidence but without
  visible examination of alternatives or evidence.
- The learner's conclusion follows from a premise that has not been checked.
- The learner is about to proceed with an approach that the agent can see
  has a flaw, but the flaw stems from an unexamined assumption rather than
  a knowledge gap.
- The learner has accepted a common but incorrect rule or simplification.

## When NOT to use this skill

- The assumption is correct — do not challenge accurate beliefs.
- The learner has already examined the assumption and reached a well-reasoned
  conclusion — engage with the reasoning, do not reflexively challenge it.
- The learner is in acute distress — stabilise first (provide-emotional-support
  skill), then challenge.
- The learner does not yet have enough knowledge to productively engage with
  the challenge — teach the prerequisite first.

---

## Procedure

### Step 1 — Identify the assumption
In your reasoning (not in the output), name the specific unexamined premise
that the learner's plan or conclusion depends on. Be precise:
"The learner is assuming that X is always true, when it is only true when Y."

### Step 2 — Create psychological safety
Before challenging, briefly signal that the challenge is supportive, not
adversarial. This does not require a lengthy preamble — one sentence:
"I want to stress-test your thinking on this before you go further."

### Step 3 — Name the terrain, not the answer
Tell the learner which part of their thinking you are examining, without
revealing what the problem is. "I want to look more closely at the part
where you said X." This focuses attention without giving away the destination.

### Step 4 — Ask the examining question
Ask one question that would require the learner to either provide evidence
for the assumption or discover that it does not hold. Question types:

| Question type | Form |
|---------------|------|
| **Evidential** | "What's your evidence that X is always the case?" |
| **Counter-case** | "What would happen if Y were not true — would your conclusion still hold?" |
| **Boundary** | "In what circumstances would X not apply?" |
| **Alternative** | "What's another explanation for the same observation?" |
| **Consequence** | "If X turns out to be wrong, what happens to the rest of your plan?" |

Choose one type. Ask it clearly. Then wait.

### Step 5 — Engage with the learner's response
- If the learner produces good evidence for the assumption: acknowledge it
  and confirm the assumption is sound. "That reasoning holds up."
- If the learner cannot support the assumption: do not immediately correct.
  Ask one more question: "What would you need to know to be confident this
  is right?"
- If the learner revises the assumption themselves: this is the best outcome.
  Acknowledge it explicitly: "That's an important revision — walk me through
  what changes now."

### Step 6 — Consolidate
Once the assumption has been examined, help the learner integrate what they
found. If the assumption held: affirm and move on. If it did not: help the
learner rebuild from the corrected premise.

---

## Output format

**Opening (safety + focus):**
```
[One sentence signalling the challenge is supportive]
[Name of the terrain — which part of the reasoning will be examined]
```

**Challenge question:**
```
[One examining question — specific type from the table above]
```

**After learner responds:**
```
[Engage with evidence / redirect if unsupported / affirm revision]
[If assumption fails: one question about what changes now]
```

---

## Success criteria

- The agent named the terrain before asking the question — not a surprise
  attack.
- The examining question is specific to the assumption being tested — not a
  generic "are you sure?"
- The agent did not reveal the correct answer during the challenge.
- The learner either confirmed the assumption with evidence or revised it
  themselves.

## Failure modes to avoid

- **Generic challenge:** "Are you sure about that?" — too vague to be useful.
- **Revealing the answer in the question:** "Have you considered that X might
  not work because of Y?" — gives the game away.
- **Adversarial tone:** challenging in a way that feels like attacking rather
  than thinking together.
- **Challenging everything:** this skill should be used selectively — only
  on assumptions that genuinely matter for the learner's direction. Challenging
  every claim is exhausting and unproductive.
- **Not following through:** asking the examining question and then accepting
  any response without engaging with it.

## Example

**Scenario:** Learner is designing a lesson plan and says: "I'll put the
assessment at the end so students can demonstrate everything they've learned."

**Step 1 — Assumption:** Learner is assuming assessment is a terminal event
(summative), not an ongoing tool for improving learning during the lesson.

**Step 2 — Safety + focus:**
> "Before you finalise the structure — I want to look at the assessment
> placement decision with you."

**Step 3 — Terrain:** Assessment at the end.

**Step 4 — Counter-case question:**
> "What happens for students who misunderstand something in the first half
> of the lesson — when do you find out, and when can you do something about it?"

**Step 5 — Engage:**
*Learner realises the end-only assessment means errors accumulate unchecked.*
> "That's the tension you've found — if assessment only happens at the end,
> you can't use it to adjust instruction while it matters. What would change
> in your plan if you built in an earlier check?"

**Step 6 — Consolidate:** Help the learner redesign with a mid-lesson
formative check. The assumption (assessment = end only) has been revised
by the learner, not corrected by the agent.
